Applied Data Privacy — Lecture 3

This self-study notebook derives differential privacy from an attacker’s decision problem. It is meant to be read and run: code is folded, every visual answers a question posed immediately before it, and the closing comparison separates what Laplace, Gaussian, and uniform noise can guarantee. For the code-hidden lecture version, open the presentation deck.

The contract: one hidden bit, two neighboring worlds

We use add/remove adjacency. Fix a target record and two neighboring datasets:

\[ D_0 = D \quad\text{(target absent)}, \qquad D_1 = D \cup \{z\} \quad\text{(target present)}. \]

The mechanism \(M\), its parameters, the data domain, and everything common to \(D_0,D_1\) are public. The private fact is the one-bit world label \(b\in\{0,1\}\). The attacker sees one released output \(O\sim M(D_b)\), knows the mechanism and the neighboring pair, and tries to infer \(b\).

The labels \(H_0\) for absent/negative and \(H_1\) for present/positive are conventions. Privacy cannot depend on which world we happen to call positive, so a differential-privacy guarantee must control both directions. This lecture studies one fixed pair to build intuition; the final definition quantifies over every allowed neighboring pair and every decision rule.

Why choose such a strong attacker?

Earlier anonymity proposals failed when their threat models guessed too little about auxiliary information. Here we make the pessimistic choice explicit: the attacker may know every public detail and all common records. Only the target’s membership bit remains hidden. This is not a claim that every real attacker is so informed. It is a way to make the promise survive future side information.

Cryptography can hide a message while authorized parties retain a secret key. A useful statistical release has no analogous escape hatch: it must depend on the dataset enough to answer a question. A constant mechanism gives perfect privacy and zero utility. We therefore need to bound how much the useful output changes the attacker’s evidence, rather than demand that it reveal nothing.

Under \(D_0\), outputs follow \(P=M(D_0)\); under \(D_1\), they follow \(Q=M(D_1)\). Membership inference has become a binary hypothesis test based on a single draw.

Question: how should one output become a guess?

A decision rule maps an observed output to \(0\) or \(1\), possibly using randomness. For these equal-variance Gaussians, a natural rule guesses \(H_1\) when \(O>\tau\). Moving \(\tau\) left makes the attacker say “present” more often in both worlds; moving it right makes that guess rarer.

With \(\tau=0.35\), which shaded region is evidence for presence? Which errors grow if the threshold moves left? Answer first, then use the visual to check the geometry.

Question: which mistakes matter?

For a fixed rule, there are four outcomes:

  • true positive: \(H_1\) generated the output and the rule guesses \(1\);
  • false negative: \(H_1\) generated the output and the rule guesses \(0\);
  • true negative: \(H_0\) generated the output and the rule guesses \(0\);
  • false positive: \(H_0\) generated the output and the rule guesses \(1\).

In privacy language, a false positive accuses an absent person of participation; a true positive correctly identifies a participant. Before viewing the panels, locate the false-positive tail under \(H_0\) and the true-positive tail under \(H_1\).

Code 
make_fpr_tpr_confusion_panels_figure(dist_neg, dist_pos, tau);

From regions to rates

The two rates we track are

\[ \operatorname{FPR}(\tau)=\Pr_{O\sim H_0}[O>\tau],\qquad \operatorname{TPR}(\tau)=\Pr_{O\sim H_1}[O>\tau]. \]

They are conditional probabilities, so they do not depend on a population membership prior. That matters: if only one person in a million participated, the strategy “always guess absent” has nearly 100% accuracy without reading the release. Accuracy would call that attack excellent even though it learned nothing. FPR and TPR expose the actual distinguishing behavior.

A deployment may care about one operating point—for example, very low FPR—but a privacy promise cannot assume every future attacker has the same costs. We need the whole tradeoff across rules.

Question: what happens when the threshold moves?

Every decision rule produces one point \((\mathrm{FPR},\mathrm{TPR})\) in the ROC plane. Randomly guessing “present” with probability \(p\) produces \((p,p)\), the diagonal. Sweeping a threshold produces a curve. A curve that bows far toward the upper-left gives the attacker high detection at low false-alarm cost; a curve near the diagonal offers little advantage.

Predict whether the shifted Gaussians lie above or below the random-guess diagonal, then inspect the analytic curve.

Code 
fpr, tpr = threshold_roc_curve(dist_neg, dist_pos)
make_analytic_roc_curve_figure(fpr, tpr);

Question: what makes the attack easier?

Keep the means at 0 and 1 and vary the shared standard deviation. Smaller noise separates the laws relative to their spread; larger noise makes them harder to distinguish. Which curve should be closest to the upper-left corner? Which should approach random guessing?

Code 
results = roc_curves_by_std(norm, 0, 1, [0.2, 0.5, 1, 2, 5])
make_analytic_roc_curves_by_std_figure(results);

The family of curves makes a crucial distinction. Noise changes an operational attack tradeoff, not merely the appearance of two density plots. At \(\sigma=0.2\), many outputs nearly identify the world. At \(\sigma=5\), the same mean shift is small relative to the noise, so the curve approaches the diagonal.

This still examines threshold rules on the observed value. Such a threshold is optimal for equal-variance Gaussians because their likelihood ratio is monotone. It need not be optimal for arbitrary distributions.

Exercise: two binomial laws

Let \(H_0=\operatorname{Bin}(5,0.5)\) and \(H_1=\operatorname{Bin}(5,0.25)\). The output is one of \(0,1,\ldots,5\).

  1. Compute \(Q(i)/P(i)\) for every output \(i\).

Notice that an upper threshold on the numeric output is not automatically correct: here smaller counts favor \(H_1\). The likelihood ratio, rather than the raw value, determines the ordering.

The Neyman–Pearson rule

For densities or probability masses \(p(o)\) under \(H_0\) and \(q(o)\) under \(H_1\), the most powerful rule at a fixed false-positive budget selects outcomes with the largest likelihood ratio

\[ \Lambda(o)=\frac{q(o)}{p(o)} \]

first. Equivalently, threshold the log-likelihood ratio \(L(o)=\log q(o)-\log p(o)\), randomizing at the boundary when necessary. This is the Neyman–Pearson lemma.

A raw-value threshold is only a convenient special case when \(L(o)\) is monotone in \(o\). Likelihood-ratio ordering handles multimodal, discrete, and support-mismatched laws without that assumption.

Back to privacy: translate every object

The statistical and privacy languages now line up exactly:

Privacy object Hypothesis-testing object
neighboring datasets \(D_0,D_1\) hypotheses \(H_0,H_1\)
output laws \(M(D_0),M(D_1)\) distributions \(P,Q\)
membership inference attack decision rule
accusation event \(S\) positive decision region
absent person accused false positive
present person detected true positive

The guarantee must hold for every neighboring pair and every measurable event \(S\), because the attacker is free to choose any rule after seeing the public mechanism. Bounding a single trained classifier, a single threshold, or an average-case pair is therefore not a differential-privacy guarantee.

Question: what shape does differential privacy impose?

If \(M\) is \((\varepsilon,\delta)\)-DP, then for every event \(S\)

\[ Q(S)\le e^\varepsilon P(S)+\delta \quad\text{and}\quad P(S)\le e^\varepsilon Q(S)+\delta. \]

For the attack event “guess present,” \(P(S)=\mathrm{FPR}\) and \(Q(S)=\mathrm{TPR}\). The first inequality gives

\[ \mathrm{TPR}\le e^\varepsilon\mathrm{FPR}+\delta. \]

Applying the reverse direction to the complementary decision supplies the reflected constraint. Before opening the explorer, predict how increasing \(\varepsilon\) or \(\delta\) enlarges the attacker’s allowed region.

The slope and shift are \(\varepsilon\) and \(\delta\)

Writing the upper line as \(\mathrm{TPR}\le m\,\mathrm{FPR}+n\), differential privacy sets \(m=e^\varepsilon\) and \(n=\delta\). Thus \(\varepsilon=\log m\) controls multiplicative distinguishability, while \(\delta\) permits a small additive exception. When \(\delta=0\), the likelihood ratio must remain bounded in both directions: this is pure DP.

The ROC statement is not a different privacy definition pasted onto DP. It is an operational restatement of the event inequalities, including both neighboring directions. A mechanism is private only if every attack ROC stays inside the bound for every allowed neighbor pair.

Question: what do noise tails and supports imply?

Hold sensitivity and displayed scale fixed, then compare Gaussian, Laplace, and uniform output laws. Ask three questions before using the explorer:

  1. Is the likelihood ratio bounded everywhere?
  2. Do the shifted laws share support?
  3. If rare outcomes have very large or infinite likelihood ratio, can a nonzero \(\delta\) cover their probability mass?

The plot answers an ROC question; it does not rank mechanisms by utility, because their loss functions and scale calibrations may differ.

Code 
PrivacyPlot(
    sensitivity=1,
    std=1.5,
    distribution_types=[norm, laplace, uniform],
).embed()

Laplace noise has exponential tails whose shifted likelihood ratio is bounded when sensitivity is bounded, making it the familiar pure-DP mechanism. Gaussian likelihood ratios grow without bound in the tails, so Gaussian noise cannot provide finite pure DP; it can provide approximate DP when \(\delta>0\) absorbs sufficiently rare tail events.

Uniform noise has bounded support. A shift creates outcomes possible in one neighboring world and impossible in the other, giving perfect evidence on a positive-probability region. Any approximate guarantee must pay at least for that unmatched mass. “Bounded noise” is therefore not the same as “bounded privacy loss.”

Summary

  • Start with a public mechanism and an add/remove neighboring pair; the private fact is one membership bit.
  • The mechanism induces two output laws, and an attacker is a binary decision rule on one output.
  • FPR and TPR describe distinguishing power without being distorted by the membership prior.
  • Sweeping rules yields an ROC curve; nearer the diagonal means less operational evidence.
  • Neyman–Pearson orders outputs by likelihood ratio, not necessarily by their numeric value.
  • \((\varepsilon,\delta)\)-DP bounds every attack ROC in both neighboring directions: \(\varepsilon\) is the log-slope and \(\delta\) the additive shift.
  • Laplace, Gaussian, and uniform noise differ because their likelihood ratios and supports differ, not simply because one density looks wider.

The next lecture reverses this perspective: instead of starting from a theorem and drawing its ROC bound, privacy auditing samples mechanism outputs, fixes an attack, and derives a statistically valid empirical lower bound on leakage.
